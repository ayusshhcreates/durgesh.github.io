Fan Writing

Rabbit holes
are my whole thing.

Durgesh Chandra Sharma — Entertainment Writing

I follow a lot of shows, movies, comics, and music, more than I probably should admit. Ask me about a Marvel character and I'll end up telling you their whole arc, the comics version, the movie version, and why the two don't agree with each other. Same with a Netflix show, I'm not just watching it, I'm reading about who wrote it, why they cast who they cast, what got cut before release.

I go down rabbit holes for fun. Give me a character's messy backstory, a show's production drama, or some late-night bit that blew up online, and I'll disappear into it for hours, not because I have to, just because I want to know everything. That curiosity ends up in my own writing eventually, one way or another.

Give me a topic and I'll show you.

Sample — Marvel

Loki Was Never Redeemed.
That's Why He Works.

Everyone loves to say Loki's arc is about redemption. It isn't, and that's the best part.

Look at what actually happens across the movies and the show. Loki doesn't become good. He becomes tired. Tired of losing, tired of being second, tired of every plan collapsing the second he trusts someone. By the time he's standing at the end of time itself, holding the whole multiverse together alone, it's not because he's transformed into a hero. It's because he finally ran out of other options that didn't involve sacrificing something.

That's not redemption. That's exhaustion turning into purpose, which is a completely different, much more interesting story.

Compare that to how Marvel usually handles this arc: villain does bad things, villain meets found family, villain learns to love, villain sacrifices self, credits roll. Loki technically hits all those beats, but the show is smart enough to never let him enjoy it. He doesn't get the family. He doesn't get Sylvie. He doesn't even get to be remembered by the people he saved. He gets a throne no one will ever visit, alone, forever, holding a job nobody else wanted.

Marvel keeps writing "redemption" as a reward. Loki's version is the only one where redemption is closer to a punishment, and somehow that's what makes it land. He didn't earn a happy ending. He earned the one ending that was actually true to who he'd always been: someone who ends up alone at the top, doing the right thing for reasons that are still, underneath it all, a little bit selfish.

That's a better character arc than "villain becomes good guy." Marvel just doesn't market it that way.
